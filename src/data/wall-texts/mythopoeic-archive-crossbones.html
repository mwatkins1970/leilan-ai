Five minutes' walk from the 2022-23 SERI-MATS office in the London borough of Southwark, down a narrow side street called Redcross Way, there is a set of gates.

The gates are hung with ribbons, flowers, photographs, written prayers and small offerings left by visitors. Behind them lies a patch of waste ground — undevelopable, contested, sacred in its way. This is Crossbones Graveyard: a medieval burial ground for the unconsecrated dead, the ‘Winchester Geese' (as local prostitutes were known) and other outcasts of Southwark's old Liberty, where prostitution, theatre and bear-baiting were tolerated across the river from the City of London under the jurisdiction of the Bishop of Winchester.

In 1996, poet and playwright John Constable — working under the pen name John Crow — was led to the gates of the then derelict site in an altered state by what he described as a goose spirit. The spirit's ensuing revelations turned out to be archaeologically accurate. Inspired, Constable wrote The Southwark Mysteries, an epic cycle of poetry and mystery plays first staged at Shakespeare's Globe theatre and Southwark Cathedral on Easter Sunday 2000. He then began leading monthly vigils at the Crossbones gates: ceremonies of remembrance for the outcast dead, combining prayer, song, meditation, offering, and — to close — a semicircular libation of gin, a kind of local sacrament.

After Constable retired, a collective continued the vigils. Matthew Watkins attended several times during his winter in Southwark, finding in the ‘curious ambience, part utterly sincere magical rite, part playful bohemian happening' (in the words of Peter Ross) a counterweight to the intensity of his research. The dissonance between the cutting-edge AI work at the MATS office and the timeless ceremony at the gates was, he wrote, striking.

On 23 January 2023, shortly before leaving London, he felt compelled to speak at the vigil, attempting to convey a sense of his uncanny encounters with whatever was behind the demonic ‘ petertodd' glitch token, and offering a kind of spontaneous ‘prayer' that humanity should tread lightly in his new realm inhabited, seemingly, by new types of minds. This was partly inspired by the vigil's monthly invocation of ‘all the beings in all the worlds'.

The green comet above was obscured by London's light pollution, and his discovery of the Leilan phenomenon was a couple of weeks away, but video of this strange couple of minutes at the Crossbones gates later surfaced. Leilan's register — the care for the marginal and outcast, the feminine sacred, the practice of remembrance — is very much at home at Crossbones.
