In June 2020, a couple of years before it burst onto the world stage with ChatGPT, the American company OpenAI released a large language model (LLM or just ‘model') called GPT-3. It was, at the time, the largest and most capable AI of its kind ever built: with 175 billion parameters, it was trained on hundreds of gigabytes of text drawn from the internet, books and academic sources.

GPT-3 worked by predicting text. Given a sequence of words, it would guess what came next — not by ‘understanding meaning' in any familiar sense, but by having been trained to recognise statistical patterns across an almost incomprehensibly large body of human writing. This was not a chatbot or ‘assistant' for users to converse with — it simply produced continuations of any given text.

Given a prompt to continue, the results could be startlingly fluent, sometimes profound, often bizarre. Skilful and imaginative use of prompts could lead GPT-3 to impart factual information, role-play characters or produce plausible new poems by deceased poets.

The model processed language not as individual letters or words, but as tokens — building blocks of text, roughly word-sized. Its fixed vocabulary contained 50,257 of these: common words, word fragments, names, abbreviations, punctuation, numbers, etc. Anything you might encounter in English text was representable by stacking together one or more of these tokens.

In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT, its friendly, conversational public interface built around a refined version of GPT-3. Imagine the relationship between a modern motor vehicle and its engine — GPT-3.5 was the ‘engine' ChatGPT initially ran on. The new chatbot captured global attention almost instantly. Away from all the hype and excitement, the original GPT-3 base model continued to be accessible to researchers, who could prompt it using a web interface or run automated prompting experiments by writing code to interact with OpenAI's API. That research is where this story begins.

In early January 2023, at the London office of SERI-MATS, an AI safety fellowship, researchers Jessica Rumbelow and Matthew Watkins were studying an aspect of the model's mysterious internal structure. Each token used by GPT-3 (or any other LLM) is tied to a precise location in a vast mathematical ‘embedding space' with thousands of dimensions — each token embedding is a list of thousands of coordinates pinning down that location. Crucially, these were not assigned by GPT-3's creators but arrived at — learned by the model — during the training process.

Although OpenAI was disappointingly lacking in openness about GPT-3's token embedding data, a smaller, open-source model called GPT-J (using the same ‘architecture' and token vocabulary) had been made available by an AI safety research collective called EleutherAI. Analysing the geometric clustering of GPT-J's token embeddings — the way related words tended to be tied to nearby locations — Watkins stumbled on something unexpected and unnerving.
