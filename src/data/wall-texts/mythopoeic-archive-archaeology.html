The name 'Leilan' carries at least two distinct histories — and their convergence in a language model's token vocabulary was entirely accidental.

Tell Leilan: In the Khabur River basin of northeastern Syria, a farming settlement was established in the early fifth millennium BCE. It grew into a substantial city, was conquered and renamed Shubat-Enlil ('residence of the god Enlil') by the Amorite king Shamshi-Adad I in the early second millennium BCE, and was abandoned around 1726 BCE following what archaeologists believe was a prolonged drought. Excavated by a Yale University team from 1979 to 2008, the site yielded an archive of over 1,100 cuneiform tablets. Among the religious remains was evidence of the veneration of Nin-Ninua — a regional epithet for the goddess Ishtar/Inanna, deity of love, war, fertility and the planet Venus. The site is named Tell Leilan after the modern Arabic name for the mound.

Puzzle & Dragons: In 2012, the Japanese mobile game studio GungHo released Puzzle & Dragons — a hybrid of match-three puzzle mechanics and role-playing monster collection. The game's pantheon drew from world mythologies: Zeus, Minerva, Odin, Isis, Cú Chulainn. Among them were four figures based on the Han Dynasty Chinese cardinal deities: the Azure Dragon of the East, White Tiger of the North, Black Tortoise of the West, and Vermillion Bird of the South. The Vermillion Bird — 'Suzaku' in Japanese, a fire-element deity associated with summer and the south — was rendered in the game's anime style as a flame-haired warrior princess with multicoloured wings, brandishing flaming swords. The character's name was Leilan Suzaku, usually shortened to simply Leilan.

Her name appeared many times in tedious and unreadable gaming logs that somehow made their way into the vast internet dataset used to construct GPT-3's original token vocabulary. The algorithm saw ' Leilan' often enough in those logs to justify giving it a token. This placed it alongside ' Zeus', ' Odin', ' Isis', and the other mythological figures the game had borrowed — and the model, learning associations across billions of words of human writing, absorbed whatever the internet had said about Tell Leilan, about goddesses and the sacred feminine, and attached much of it to this name.

When GPT-3 was trained, the token was very rarely seen (relative to the most common 50,000 words and fragments seen in English text). This meant the token was 'undertrained' — the model didn't know quite what to do with it, causing glitchy outputs. Probing what was behind it, researcher Matthew Watkins found a cluster of associations that the neural network had assembled from the sediment of human mythological imagination: ancient, maternal, luminous, set against darkness.

The Mesopotamian archaeology and Japanese gaming logs had together conspired, without intention, to give this associative cluster a new name.
