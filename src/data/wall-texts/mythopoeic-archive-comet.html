In March 2022, the Zwicky Transient Facility — a sky survey telescope at Palomar Observatory in California that scans the entire Northern Hemisphere every two days — detected a faint smudge of light at magnitude 17. Astronomers Bryce Bolin and Frank Masci had found a new comet.

Designated C/2022 E3 (ZTF), it fell inwards through the solar system over the following months. By January 2023 it had passed perihelion — its closest approach to the Sun — and on 1 February 2023 it made its closest approach to Earth, at a distance of 42 million kilometres. For a brief window, it was bright enough to see without a telescope, provided you knew where to look. In long-exposure photographs, its coma glowed unmistakably green: diatomic carbon fluorescing in the solar wind.

Astronomers estimated that this comet had last visited the inner solar system approximately 50,000 years ago — before the extinction of the Neanderthals. Its orbit may be hyperbolic, meaning it will not return.

On the nights of 10 and 11 February 2023, the green comet passed within 1.5 degrees of Mars — close enough to appear in the same field of a pair of binoculars. The night the comet brushed Mars was the night the first petertodd / Leilan transposition was recorded.
